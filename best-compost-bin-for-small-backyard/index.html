Which setup would we choose?

Consider the GEOBIN if you have a patch of ground, leaves to compost, and room to turn a pile. For a paved space or recurring rodent problems, investigate an enclosed system first. If your main goal is handling kitchen scraps with very little outdoor space, check local collection before buying a bin.

Measure the working space, not just the bin

Put a hose or a length of string on the ground to mark the bin's outline. Stand beside it with the tool you would use to turn compost. Work out where the lid, a bucket, and finished material would go. A circle that fits between a fence and a path may still be awkward to use.

Yardfully's detailed specification sheet lists the GEOBIN at 36 inches high, with a 24–44 inch diameter and a nominal capacity range of 70–246 gallons. Narrowing the ring reduces its capacity. The maximum volume does not apply at every setting.

A practical choice for a narrow yard

If you have a 30-inch-wide gap, the IM4000's listed width fills it before you account for your hands or the door. We would measure a more accessible position before ordering. If you have more ground space and a seasonal pile of leaves, an adjustable open ring may suit the volume better. That judgment follows the published sizes and access requirements; it does not establish that either product is easier to use.

When another approach makes more sense

Consider the FCMP Outdoor IM4000 for separate batches

Its divided drum lets one chamber finish while the other takes new material. FCMP's current listing specifies approximately 43 US gallons total across both chambers, not per side. Confirm the model and capacity on the seller's listing: older retail descriptions may differ. We have not measured loaded turning effort, door clearance, composting speed or pest resistance.

The instructions show emptying onto a small tarp beneath the stand: rotate the door downward, uncover the finished chamber, then pull the tarp out. Plan an exit path for that tarp. The manual does not specify a numerical door-opening or emptying clearance, so check it with the seller if space is tight.

Check local collection if kitchen scraps are your main concern

EPA describes both curbside services and drop-off sites. Ask your city, county, or waste provider what is available at your address and what they accept. A countertop caddy stores scraps for transfer; it does not make finished compost. Decide how often you can empty it before buying a larger container.

Plan for upkeep and pests

Make room for dry material. EPA recommends two to three volumes of browns, such as dry leaves, per volume of greens. That supply needs storage space too.
Cover food scraps. EPA advises burying them beneath 4–8 inches of browns. Keep meat, dairy, grease, and pet waste out of a backyard pile.
Do not assume an open bin excludes rodents. EPA recommends a lid, potentially a floor, and no gaps larger than ¼ inch. An unmodified GEOBIN does not provide that enclosure.
Keep moisture and air in balance. Damp material and occasional turning support decomposition. A wet, compacted pile needs attention regardless of the container.

Source: EPA: Composting at home.

Common questions

How soon will compost be ready?

EPA gives roughly 3–5 months for a well-maintained pile and up to a year when untended. That is guidance, not a GEOBIN performance guarantee. Ingredients, moisture, air, and temperature affect progress. See EPA's harvesting guidance.

Can I put a GEOBIN on a balcony?

We would not choose this open, ground-based design for a balcony. Consider your available drainage, building rules, and weight restrictions before selecting any setup there. Collection may be the simpler option.

Do I need to buy a bin at all?

Not necessarily. Before ordering, check whether a local program accepts your scraps or offers subsidized equipment. Yardfully describes municipal bin programs; availability and eligibility depend on where you live.

Before you buy

Mark out the footprint and working area, decide where dry leaves will be stored, and check for recurring pest activity. If those checks support an open pile, the GEOBIN is worth considering. If they do not, choose the approach that fits your yard and routine.
